About VizBible

VizBible turns the Bible’s timelines, journeys and numbers into pictures you can explore. It is built for students, pastors, teachers and anyone who wants to see how Scripture fits together: where Paul travelled, what Jesus did in each place, which letters were written when, and how the kings of Israel and Judah followed one another.

What you will find

  • Paul’s journeys, Jesus’ journeys and Old Testament journeys, with ancient and modern place names and Bible references for every stop.
  • A New Testament timeline of all 27 books, with the churches Paul wrote to.
  • The kings of Israel and Judah, books by date of writing and a book-by-book dashboard.

About the dates and places

Routes on the maps are approximate, and some sites are disputed; the maps say so where that is the case. Please check the Scripture references for yourself. The aim is to honour the Word of God with careful, reverent presentation.

How the New Testament books are dated

No New Testament book carries a date, so every date on this site is an estimate given as a range. The site shows two sets of dates, and you can switch between them on the New Testament timeline and the chronology table.

  • Conservative dates (the default). These accept the traditional authors named in the books and in early church tradition, and they read the internal evidence at face value. For example, Acts ends with Paul still under arrest in Rome, so Acts is placed around AD 62–65, and Luke, which Acts calls the “former treatise” (Acts 1:1), is placed just before it. On these dates most books are written before Jerusalem’s temple was destroyed in AD 70.
  • Critical dates. These follow the mainstream view in university biblical studies. It dates the Gospels and Acts mostly after AD 70, and treats some letters (such as Ephesians, Colossians, 2 Thessalonians, 1–2 Timothy, Titus and 2 Peter) as written after the apostle’s lifetime.
  • What both agree on. Paul’s seven undisputed letters (Romans, 1–2 Corinthians, Galatians, Philippians, 1 Thessalonians and Philemon) get the same dates in both views, roughly AD 48–60. Two fixed points anchor Paul’s timeline: the Gallio inscription found at Delphi, which dates Gallio’s year as governor of Achaia to about AD 51–52 (Acts 18:12), and the death of Herod Agrippa I in AD 44 (Acts 12), recorded by Josephus.

The ranges on this site are rounded summaries of the positions in standard introductions to the New Testament, such as:

  • Conservative: D. A. Carson and Douglas J. Moo, An Introduction to the New Testament (2nd ed., 2005); Donald Guthrie, New Testament Introduction (revised ed., 1990).
  • Critical: Raymond E. Brown, An Introduction to the New Testament (1997); Bart D. Ehrman, The New Testament: A Historical Introduction to the Early Christian Writings.
  • Early dating from outside the conservative camp: John A. T. Robinson, Redating the New Testament (1976), which argued that every New Testament book was written before AD 70.

Individual scholars within each view differ, sometimes by a decade or more, so treat every range as approximate.
